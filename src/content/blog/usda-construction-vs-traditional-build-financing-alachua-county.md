## Building in Alachua County: Two Paths, Very Different Experiences

You have found a parcel outside Gainesville — maybe closer to Newberry, Archer, or one of the quieter communities that make Alachua County appealing for people who want space without sacrificing proximity to a city. You want to build, not buy. Now comes the question that trips up a lot of aspiring homebuilders: which financing path actually makes sense?

There are two main routes worth understanding: the USDA construction loan and traditional build financing. Each one has a distinct structure, a different set of costs, and a different set of requirements. Neither is a perfect fit for everyone, but knowing how they compare puts you in a much stronger position when you sit down with a lender.

---

## What a USDA Construction Loan Actually Is

The USDA Single Close Construction loan combines two things that traditional financing keeps separate: the short-term loan that funds the construction itself and the long-term mortgage you carry once the home is finished. With this approach, you close once, lock your terms once, and avoid the hassle of qualifying a second time after your home is built.

For eligible borrowers in Alachua County, one of the most notable features is the zero-down-payment option. Rural areas of the county — many of which sit outside the USDA-designated urban boundaries — may qualify for this program. That means someone building on a rural lot might be able to finance the full construction cost without a large cash down payment.

USDA loans also carry government-backed underwriting guidelines that differ from conventional products. Income limits apply, and the property must be in an eligible rural or semi-rural location. ## How Traditional Construction Financing Works

Traditional construction loans have been the industry standard for decades, and they are well-understood by most builders and title companies. Here is the basic structure: you take out a short-term construction loan — often with interest-only payments during the build — and then, once the certificate of occupancy is issued, you refinance into or convert to a permanent mortgage.

That two-step process has real implications. You will go through underwriting twice. You will pay closing costs twice in most cases. And your permanent financing terms are determined at a different point in time than your construction financing terms, which introduces a layer of uncertainty.

For borrowers who do not meet USDA income or location requirements, traditional construction financing is often the primary option. It also tends to offer more flexibility around property types and builder requirements. Some borrowers simply prefer the separation between the build phase and the permanent loan, which can allow adjustments along the way.

With construction material costs remaining a point of concern in 2025 — and housing supply pressures showing no signs of easing in north-central Florida — understanding the cost structure of your financing matters more than ever when budgeting a new build.

## Side-by-Side: Key Differences That Matter in Alachua County

Here is a plain comparison of how the two approaches differ for a borrower building in Alachua County:

**Down payment:** USDA construction may allow zero down for eligible borrowers. Traditional construction typically requires a meaningful down payment, often ranging from ten to twenty percent of the total project cost.

**Closing costs:** USDA closes once. Traditional construction financing typically means two closings, which can add thousands in fees and third-party costs.

**Location requirements:** USDA requires the property to be in an eligible rural area. Outer areas of Alachua County often meet this standard; parcels closer to central Gainesville may not.

**Income limits:** USDA loans cap household income based on county and family size. Traditional financing does not carry these restrictions.

**Builder approval:** USDA requires the builder to be approved through the lender. Traditional lenders vary in how strictly they vet builders.

**Timeline:** USDA construction loans may take longer to process initially due to additional government review steps. Traditional construction loans can sometimes move faster at the front end.

## What Alachua County Borrowers Should Know About Eligibility

Alachua County has a genuinely mixed character. Gainesville brings a university city energy, with denser neighborhoods and established infrastructure. But travel twenty or thirty minutes in nearly any direction and the landscape opens up into the kind of rural acreage that the USDA program was designed to support.

Communities like Newberry, High Springs, Alachua, and Micanopy sit in areas that may fall within USDA rural eligibility boundaries, though exact eligibility is determined by the USDA's current property maps and is subject to change. If you are considering a lot in one of these areas, it is worth verifying eligibility before you fall in love with a parcel.

Household income limits also apply. The USDA sets these by county and family size, and they are adjusted periodically. A household that earns too much to qualify for USDA may still have strong options through conventional construction financing — it just changes the math around down payments and structure.

## How to Decide Which Path Fits Your Situation

Start with three questions:

1. Is the property in a USDA-eligible area?
2. Does your household income fall within USDA limits for Alachua County?
3. Do you have a builder who is willing to go through lender approval?

If the answer to all three is yes, a USDA construction loan may be worth exploring seriously. The single-close structure and potential zero-down feature can make a meaningful difference in how much cash you need at the start of the process.

If the property is near Gainesville's urban core, or if your income exceeds USDA limits, traditional construction financing is likely your primary path. In that case, understanding the two-close structure and building closing costs into your budget from the beginning is important.
