## How Flight Attendant Pay Gets Documented

Most flight attendants are paid on a combination of flight hours, per diem allowances, and sometimes premium pay for international or holiday trips. Some also earn from pick-up trips, override pay, or purser differentials. Each component is real income — but not all of it is treated the same way by underwriters.

Here is what typically counts toward qualifying income:

- **Base pay and guaranteed flight hours** — This is the most straightforward component.
- **Trip-for-trip variable pay** — Averaged over 24 months, this often does count, as long as you can show it has been consistent.
- **Bonuses and incentive pay** — These may count if there is a two-year history showing they are likely to continue.

Here is what often does not count:

- **Per diem** — This is an expense reimbursement, not income, in the eyes of most lenders. It will not usually be added to your qualifying total.
- **One-time signing bonuses** — Generally excluded unless recurring.

Bringing your most recent two years of W-2s, your current pay stubs, and a copy of your union contract (if applicable) can help your loan officer build an accurate income picture. ## Reserve Status and Employment Gaps

Flight attendants who are new to a carrier, returning from a furlough, or recently recalled from involuntary leave sometimes worry that any employment gap disqualifies them. In many cases, it does not — as long as the gap is explained and the broader employment history shows stability in the aviation industry.

Recent industry news has highlighted how unsettled cabin crew employment can be, particularly around labor negotiations and potential work stoppages. If you have experienced time away from the line due to a strike, furlough, or contract dispute, documenting the reason clearly and showing your return-to-work date often satisfies underwriter questions.

The key is a paper trail. Keep your recall letter, your return-to-work confirmation, and any union communications that establish the timeline. These documents can make an otherwise complicated file straightforward.

## Loan Programs That May Work for Cabin Crew

Not every mortgage product is well-suited to the way flight attendants earn. Here is a general look at what is available:

**Conventional loans** may work well if you have a solid two-year employment history with the same airline and consistent W-2 income. They often allow for lower down payments with private mortgage insurance.

**FHA loans** are sometimes a fit for first-time buyers who have strong employment history but a smaller down payment saved. Down payments on FHA loans can be as low as 3.5%.

**VA loans** may be available if you have qualifying military service in addition to your aviation career. Not all flight attendants will have this path open, but it is worth confirming.

**Bank statement loans** are a non-QM option that may help in cases where W-2 income appears lower than your actual cash flow — though these programs have their own qualification criteria.

## What to Prepare Before You Apply

Getting organized early removes most of the stress from the mortgage process. Here is a practical checklist for flight attendants:

- Two most recent years of W-2s from all employers
- Most recent 30 days of pay stubs
- Most recent two months of bank statements (all pages)
- Current employment contract or union agreement, if available
- Documentation of any employment gaps, furloughs, or recalls
- Records of any additional income sources (second jobs, rental income)
- Government-issued ID
