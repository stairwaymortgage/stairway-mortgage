## What the One-Time Close Structure Actually Means

Most construction financing splits into two phases: a short-term construction loan that pays your builder during the build, and a permanent mortgage you close into once the home is complete. That means two applications, two sets of closing costs, and two rounds of paperwork.

A One-Time Close loan collapses that into a single transaction. You close once before construction begins. That single closing covers the construction period and automatically converts to your permanent loan when the home is finished. You work with one lender, sign one set of documents, and — in many cases — face fewer surprises at the finish line.

For buyers in Alachua County who are building on rural or rural-adjacent land, pairing this structure with USDA financing adds another layer of simplicity: the zero down payment requirement. You could build a new home without the large upfront cash outlay that most construction scenarios demand.

## How USDA Eligibility Works in Alachua County

Alachua County has a split personality, and that actually works in your favor if you are considering this program.

Gainesville, the county seat, is a mid-sized university city with dense neighborhoods that generally fall outside USDA geographic eligibility. But Alachua County stretches well beyond Gainesville's city limits. Communities like Newberry, Alachua, High Springs, Micanopy, and Waldo sit in areas that may fall within USDA-designated rural zones — though eligibility is determined parcel by parcel, not by city name alone.

The USDA also sets income limits for this program. These limits are based on household size and the median income for the area. Many working families in Alachua County fall within those thresholds, but it is worth verifying your specific situation before you fall in love with a lot.

## The Construction Timeline: What to Expect

One reason homebuyers hesitate to pursue construction financing is the fear of complexity. The timeline is longer than a standard purchase, and there are more moving parts. But understanding the sequence can make it feel manageable.

Here is a general picture of how the process unfolds:

**Before closing:** You select your lot, choose a USDA-approved builder, finalize your plans and budget, and go through the loan approval process. The lender reviews your builder's credentials, the construction contract, and your financial profile.

**At closing:** You sign once. The construction phase begins. Funds are disbursed to your builder in draws as work progresses and inspections confirm completion milestones.

**After construction:** When the home passes final inspection and the certificate of occupancy is issued, the loan automatically converts to your permanent mortgage. No second closing. No new application.

The entire construction period is typically capped at a set number of months, so working with a builder who has experience with government-backed construction loans matters. ## What You Need to Bring to the Table

Zero down does not mean zero preparation. USDA One-Time Close loans have specific requirements that you will want to understand before you start.

**Credit:** A minimum credit score is required, and the threshold for USDA programs is generally in the mid-600s, though individual lender overlays may vary. If your score needs some work, starting the conversation early gives you time to address it.

**Income documentation:** Because this is a government-backed program with income limits, you will document your household income thoroughly. W-2s, tax returns, and pay stubs are standard.

**Builder approval:** Your builder must be licensed, insured, and approved by the lender. This protects you as much as it protects the program. A builder who has worked with USDA construction loans before is a significant advantage.

**The lot:** If you already own land, that equity may factor into the transaction. If you are purchasing a lot as part of the build, that cost is typically rolled into the total loan amount.
