## Why Real Estate Plays a Central Role in Family Wealth

Across many American families, real estate is the largest single asset they hold. That is true in Alachua County as much as anywhere. Whether it is a home near the University of Florida in Gainesville, a property in a rural pocket outside Newberry, or a small piece of land that has been in the family for decades, real estate tends to carry both financial and emotional weight.

Unlike a savings account, real property is tangible. It can generate rental income, serve as a primary residence, or be held and transferred to the next generation. When approached with intention, a well-chosen property may grow in value over time and create a foundation that outlasts the original buyer.

But that only works if the financing, ownership structure, and estate plan are all aligned. Most families focus on the purchase and leave the rest for later. Legacy-minded families do the opposite.

## The Mortgage Piece Most Families Skip

When families think about generational wealth and real estate, they often focus on ownership. Who holds the title. What the will says. Whether there is a trust.

What gets overlooked is the mortgage itself.

If a home is still carrying a loan when it is passed to the next generation, the heir faces choices. In some cases, depending on the loan type and lender, they may be able to assume the existing loan. In others, they will need to refinance. And in situations where the property is underwater or the estate is complicated, they may need to sell — often at a price point that reflects urgency rather than value.

None of those outcomes are automatically bad. But none of them should come as a surprise. Families who plan ahead know what kind of mortgage is on each property, what happens to that loan at death, and how the estate is structured to handle it. That kind of clarity protects the asset.

## What a Thoughtful Strategy Might Include

Every family's situation in Alachua County is different, but a generational wealth plan that includes real estate often touches several areas:

**Ownership structure.** How the property is titled — individually, jointly, in a trust, or through an LLC — affects what happens to it and how it transfers. A mortgage professional and an estate attorney should both weigh in here.

**Loan type and terms.** Some loan structures are more transferable than others. Understanding what you have and what options exist is part of planning responsibly.

**Equity access.** Families sometimes use equity in an existing property to help a younger generation purchase their own home. This can be a meaningful way to extend wealth across a generation without liquidating the original asset.

**Clear documentation.** Inherited homes frequently get sold — not because the family wanted to sell, but because no one knew what the plan was. Written, shared plans reduce that risk.
