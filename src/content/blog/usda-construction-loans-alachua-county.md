## How the USDA Construction-to-Permanent Loan Works

Most conventional construction loans have two phases: a short-term construction loan while the home is being built, followed by a separate closing to convert that into a traditional mortgage. That means two sets of paperwork, two rounds of closing costs, and two approval processes.

The USDA construction-to-permanent loan is designed to combine those phases into a single closing. You close once before construction begins. The loan covers the building phase, and when your home is complete, it transitions into your long-term mortgage automatically — without another closing.

This structure could reduce the total cost of financing and simplify the overall timeline. For a first-time buyer coordinating a builder, a lot purchase, and a move, reducing complexity matters.

## Who May Be Eligible in Alachua County

Eligibility for a USDA construction loan has two main dimensions: the borrower and the property.

**Borrower eligibility** generally requires that your household income falls within USDA's limits for the area, that you intend to occupy the home as your primary residence, and that you meet credit and debt guidelines. These limits are set at the county level and are updated periodically, so the numbers you find in a general search may not reflect what applies to Alachua County today.

**Property eligibility** depends on location. USDA maps define which areas are considered rural or suburban enough to qualify. Alachua County has a mix: Gainesville itself is largely ineligible, but many surrounding areas — including parts of Newberry, Waldo, Hawthorne, and unincorporated communities — may fall within eligible zones. The maps do change, so verifying the specific parcel is an important early step.

The home you build must also meet USDA's modest housing standards, which include limits on features considered luxury items. Your builder must be licensed and approved through the lender's process.

## What the Process Tends to Look Like

Building a home has more moving parts than buying an existing one, and a USDA construction loan adds a layer of program requirements on top of that. Being organized early makes a real difference.

Here's a general outline of how the process often unfolds:

1. **Pre-qualification** — Before you commit to a lot or a builder, understanding your borrowing range helps you make realistic decisions.
2. **Property and location check** — Confirming the land is in a USDA-eligible area is a foundational step.
3. **Builder approval** — The lender will need to vet your contractor. Many local builders in Alachua County are already familiar with this process, but it's worth confirming early.
4. **Plans and cost breakdown** — USDA and the construction lender will review your building plans and a detailed cost estimate.
5. **Single closing** — Once everything is in order, you close once. Funds are disbursed to the builder in draws as construction milestones are reached.
6. **Transition to permanent financing** — When the certificate of occupancy is issued and the build is complete, the loan converts to your long-term mortgage.

## Common Questions Before You Start

Buyers often come to us with a few recurring concerns. Here are honest answers to the ones that come up most often:

**Do I have to own the land first?** Not necessarily. In some cases, the lot purchase can be rolled into the construction loan. This varies by situation and lender guidelines, so it's worth asking early.

**What if my credit isn't perfect?** USDA loans have more flexibility than some conventional programs in this area. You may still have options worth exploring, depending on your full financial picture.

**Can I act as my own general contractor?** In most cases, no. USDA construction loans typically require a licensed, third-party contractor.

**How long does construction need to take?** USDA construction loans generally have a defined construction period, often around twelve months. Your builder's timeline needs to fit within that window.
